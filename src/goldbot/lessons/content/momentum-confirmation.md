---

## What it is

Wait for the market to do something before acting. Here: buy only when today's
close is the highest close of the last 20 days.

You are deliberately paying a worse price than you could have got last week, in
exchange for evidence. A breakout is not a forecast that price will continue —
it is a statement that, at this moment, buyers are willing to pay more than
anyone has been willing to pay recently. That is a fact rather than an opinion,
which is more than most entry signals can claim.

## When it works

Breakouts work when moves persist, and moves persist more often than a random
walk would suggest. The mechanism is not mysterious: information spreads
slowly, large positions take weeks to build, and other trend followers are
watching the same levels you are.

The pattern that pays is asymmetric. Most breakouts fail and produce a small
loss at the stop. A few keep going and produce a large gain. The strategy is
profitable because of the shape of that distribution, not because the signal is
often right — it typically is not. Expect a win rate well under half.

## When it fails

**Choppy ranges eat breakout systems alive.** In a sideways market, the highest
close of 20 days occurs at the top of the range, which is the worst possible
place to buy. You get stopped out, price returns to the top of the range, you
buy again. This is the single most common way a breakout strategy loses money,
and the trend filter exists mainly to reduce it.

**False breakouts on thin volume.** A new high made on light participation
often reverses immediately. This system does not filter on volume, which is a
known simplification.

**Gap-up breakouts.** When the market opens sharply higher on news, the
breakout triggers at a price far above where the stop logic assumed. The
position gets sized against a stop distance that no longer reflects the risk
actually being taken.

## In gold

Gold's breakouts have a distinctive character: they cluster around macro
events — central bank policy shifts, inflation surprises, geopolitical shocks —
rather than arriving smoothly. That means a disproportionate share of signals
fire on days when something has just happened, which is also when spreads widen
and slippage is worst.

It also means the event blackout rule and this rule are in direct tension: the
best breakouts and the most dangerous days are frequently the same days. There
is no clean answer. The system's answer is to stand aside on *scheduled*
events, where the market knows something is coming and prices are unstable for
reasons unrelated to trend, while accepting unscheduled shocks as part of the
cost of participating.

A 20-day window is short enough to catch gold's faster moves and long enough to
ignore ordinary daily noise. It is not magic and it was not optimised.
